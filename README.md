This repository contains all the data files and code associated with the manuscript, "Variation in marine resource consumption among terrestrial mammals along wild and suburban areas of California coastline"

Authors: Grace B. Lewin (1), Ellen M. Dymit (2), Jennifer Allen (2), Taal Levi (2),  Elizabeth H.T. Hiroyasu (3), Charles A. Braman (1),  A. Rosie Manner (1), Hillary S. Young * (1)

(2) Department of Fisheries, Wildlife, and Conservation Sciences, Oregon State University, Corvallis, OR, USA
(3) The Nature Conservancy, California Chapter, Sacramento, CA, USA
* Corresponding author: hillary.young@ucsb.edu

ABSTRACT:
Although intact wild coastlines are increasingly rare, their ecological importance to terrestrial wildlife remains poorly understood. Notably, many terrestrial mammals forage on marine resources opportunistically, with potential cascading consequences for terrestrial communities and nutrient flow across land-sea boundaries and yet baseline data on this behavior, and on how human activity disrupts it, are largely absent. Here we used fecal DNA metabarcoding to characterize the diets of three common large mammals (coyote, bobcat, wild boar) at two coastal reserves along the central California coastline that differ markedly in human recreational intensity: the remote, access-restricted Jack and Laura Dangermond Preserve and the suburban Coal Oil Point Reserve. Using vertebrate- and invertebrate-specific primers, we asked how diets differed among species, to what extent each consumed marine prey, and how distance to coast and human activity affected marine foraging frequency. Diet richness of animal prey was 2–3 times greater for coyotes than for bobcats or wild boar. Coyote and bobcat diets were modestly similar, but wild boar diets were strongly dissimilar from both and bobcat and boar shared no prey items. Despite all three species being regularly detected on beaches, only coyotes consumed marine prey, primarily Brandt's cormorant (Urile penicillatus), pelagic cormorant (Urile pelagicus), and California sea lion (Zalophus californianus), comprising ~18% of prey detections at the remote site. Most notably, marine foraging was entirely absent at the suburban reserve — even though the species persisted there and both terrestrial and coastal ecosystems remained protected — suggesting that human recreational presence alone, apart from habitat loss, may be sufficient to suppress this behavior. Notably within the fully protected reserve, distance to coast had modest effect on marine prey detection, with most marine-derived subsidies found within 1km from coast. Cumulatively our  results indicate that human disturbance can sever land-sea trophic linkages even within formally protected areas, with potential consequences for nutrient dynamics and predator ecology across broader coastal landscapes.
